DESCRIPTION FULL (200-500 words):
The 2026 Roth IRA has a direct-contribution phaseout. Single filers above $168,000 of modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) can't contribute directly. Married filing jointly above $252,000 — same door closed. The IRS counts MAGI, not your salary (per Pub 590-A).

The backdoor Roth is a legal two-step workaround:
1. Contribute after-tax money to a traditional IRA (a non-deductible contribution — no tax deduction going in).
2. Convert that traditional IRA to a Roth IRA. The conversion is tax-free because you already paid the tax on the contribution.

The gotcha: the pro-rata rule (IRC §408(d)(2)). If you have other pre-tax IRA money (Traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRAs), the IRS aggregates your pre-tax balances when you convert. The conversion becomes partly taxable in proportion to your pre-tax balance. Example: if 30% of your aggregate IRA balance is pre-tax, 30% of the conversion is taxable — even if your $7,000 backdoor contribution was all after-tax.

Workaround to the pro-rata rule: roll all pre-tax IRA balances into your 401(k) first (if the plan accepts rollovers), so your only remaining IRA is the backdoor one. Then the pro-rata aggregation is zero.

The backdoor Roth is one of the few tax arbitrage strategies the IRS has explicitly blessed (PLR 201333416 et seq.). It's not a loophole — it's a deliberate path for high earners who can't contribute directly.

File Form 8606 every year you do a non-deductible contribution or a conversion — that form is the paper trail that proves to the IRS you already paid the tax going in.
