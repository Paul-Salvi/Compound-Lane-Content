DESCRIPTION FULL (200-500 words):
Most self-employed workers default to a SEP IRA because it's the easiest to set up. But SEP is pre-tax only. Skipping the Solo 401(k) means skipping the Roth deferral and the mega-backdoor — and leaving a six-figure tax loss on the table over thirty years.

The four accounts (2026):
- Solo 401(k) — up to $72,000/yr (IRC §415(c)). Employee deferral $24,500 + employer profit-sharing + after-tax. Has Roth.
- SEP IRA — up to ~25% of net SE income, max ~$70,000. Pre-tax only. No Roth option.
- SIMPLE IRA — up to ~$16,500 (2026). Pre-tax or Roth (since 2023 SECURE 2.0).
- Roth IRA — $7,500. Income phaseout at $153K-$168K single.

The funding order:
1. Solo 401(k) employee deferral: $24,500 (pre-tax OR Roth).
2. Roth IRA: $7,500 (if income allows).
3. Solo 401(k) employer profit-sharing: up to 25% of net SE income. Pre-tax by default, can be designated Roth if the plan allows.
4. SEP IRA as backup if no Solo 401(k) (simpler admin, lower limit).

The trap:
- Solo 401(k) = $24,500 deferral + ~$47,500 profit-sharing = $72,000.
- SEP IRA = ~$70,000 max, but ALL pre-tax.
- The $24,500 employee deferral has the Roth option. SEP does not.
- Skipping the Solo 401(k) means skipping the Roth deferral. That's a six-figure tax loss over 30 years.

The strategy:
- Open the Solo 401(k) by Sept 30 of the year you want to start.
- Max the employee deferral first (Roth if your tax rate is rising).
- Then fund the Roth IRA.
- Then sweep employer profit-sharing into the Solo 401(k) (Roth if the plan document allows).
- Use SEP IRA only if the Solo 401(k) isn't available (e.g., no full-time employees other than spouse, or you have employees and don't want the Roth conversion complications).
