DESCRIPTION FULL (200-500 words):
Married couples don't get the single-filer window. They get a smaller one. For 2026, the MFJ phase-out runs from $242,000 to $252,000 of modified adjusted gross income (MAGI). That's a $10,000 window — about two-thirds the width of the single filer's $15,000 window.

The line that matters is MAGI, not household salary. The IRS Pub 590-A Worksheet 2-2 takes your combined AGI, adds back a few items, and that's the MAGI for Roth contribution purposes. The phase-out is computed on joint MAGI.

Inside the window, the IRS worksheet reduces your allowed Roth contribution. Above $252,000, the door closes — neither spouse can contribute directly to a Roth IRA. Zero. The phase-out ends, the direct-contribution door closes for both of you.

What if you're both above the ceiling? The spousal backdoor Roth is the workaround. Each spouse opens a non-deductible traditional IRA, then each converts to a Roth. Same end account, separate on-ramps. The pro-rata rule can complicate this — see that separate reel.

Why this matters: a couple at $242,000 of MAGI gets a partial contribution per spouse, capped at a sliding fraction of the $7,500 limit. A couple at $252,000+ of MAGI gets nothing directly — but they still have the backdoor path. Plan ahead; the IRS doesn't prorate after the fact.

Why the smaller window? The MFJ range is narrower than single because the IRS gives partial credit for the second earner in the standard deduction but doesn't fully credit the second earner in the Roth phase-out. A single filer at the same income gets a wider glide path; a couple gets a shorter one.
