DESCRIPTION FULL (200-500 words):
The 2026 Roth IRA limit is $7,500 per person, per year (per IRS IR-2025-111). Add the $1,100 catch-up if you're fifty or older. Now run the math: $7,500 every year for 30 years at a 7% real return.

Future value of an annuity formula: FV = PMT × [((1 + r)^n - 1) / r]. With PMT = $7,500, r = 0.07, n = 30: FV ≈ $708,000.

Of that $708,000, you contributed $225,000. The remaining $483,000 is tax-free compound growth — more than double your contributions. The Roth is a tax-free compounding engine. That's the whole point.

Why 7% real return: that's the S&P 500 historical real (inflation-adjusted) return from 1928 to 2025 (Damodaran, NYU Stern, January 2026 update). Past performance doesn't guarantee future results, but the long-run average is the right baseline for a 30-year horizon.

What this means in practice: a 25-year-old who maxes a Roth for 30 years accumulates ~$708,000 in today's dollars, all tax-free at withdrawal. A 35-year-old who waits 10 years and only compounds for 20 years accumulates ~$308,000. The 10-year delay costs $400,000 — that's the cost of waiting.

This is also why "start early" is the most repeated advice in personal finance. Time in market is the dominant lever — not contribution amount, not market timing, not fund selection. The next 10 years of compounding are the most valuable 10 years of your financial life.

The tax-free part: with a Roth, that $708,000 is yours at fifty-nine and a half. No capital gains tax, no ordinary income tax, no required minimum distributions during your lifetime. With a taxable brokerage, the same $7,500/yr would grow to a smaller number after 15-20% long-term capital gains tax on the growth.
