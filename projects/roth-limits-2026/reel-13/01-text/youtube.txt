DESCRIPTION FULL (200-500 words):
Roth IRA withdrawals follow a fixed, IRS-mandated order. The order is:
1. Regular contributions first — always tax-free, always penalty-free.
2. Conversions on a first-in, first-out (FIFO) basis, oldest first.
3. Earnings last.

The order matters because of two separate 5-year rules (per IRC §408A(d)) and the 10% early-withdrawal penalty.

The $20,000 example. Say your Roth holds $15,000 in regular contributions and $5,000 in earnings. You withdraw $20,000. The first $15,000 is treated as a return of your contributions — no tax, no penalty, no question. The next $5,000 is treated as earnings. If you're under 59½ and no exception applies, that $5,000 is hit with ordinary income tax plus the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. On a 24% marginal bracket, that's $1,200 income tax + $500 penalty = $1,700 gone.

The 5-year rule for conversions. Each Roth conversion starts its own 5-year clock. The clock is per-conversion, not per-Roth. Convert in 2023, withdraw the converted amount in 2025 — that's under 5 years. If you're under 59½, the 10% penalty applies even though the conversion itself was tax-free. The age-59½ exception does not waive the 5-year rule.

Track it on Form 8606. Form 8606 Part III tracks your non-deductible contributions and conversions year by year. Without it, the IRS assumes every dollar in your Roth is earnings — which means every withdrawal is taxable until proven otherwise. File 8606 every year you do a conversion or a non-deductible contribution.

The bottom line: contributions are always free. Conversions are free if you wait 5 years and you're 59½. Earnings are last, and they're the bucket that triggers tax and penalty.
