DESCRIPTION FULL (200-500 words):
The backdoor Roth has one serious gotcha: the pro-rata rule (IRC §408(d)(2)).

The pro-rata rule says: when you convert a traditional IRA to a Roth, the IRS treats the conversion as a proportional mix of pre-tax and after-tax money — based on your aggregate traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances on December 31 of the conversion year. The IRS does not look at the specific account you convert.

The $90,000 example:
- Pre-tax traditional IRA: $90,000
- After-tax backdoor contribution: $7,000
- Total IRA balance: $97,000
- Pro-rata pre-tax ratio: 90,000 / 97,000 = ~92.78%
- Pro-rata after-tax ratio: 7,000 / 97,000 = ~7.22%

You convert the $7,000 to a Roth. Under pro-rata, the conversion is 92.78% pre-tax — so $7,000 × 0.9278 = $6,495 is taxable as ordinary income (at your marginal rate). The "non-deductible" part vanishes. The backdoor saves you $505 of tax on a $7,000 conversion — instead of the full $7,000 × marginal rate.

The fix:
1. Roll the $90,000 pre-tax IRA into your 401(k) — most plans accept incoming rollovers (verify with HR).
2. Wait until the rollover settles (this can take 1-2 weeks; the December 31 measurement date is critical).
3. Now your only remaining IRA is the $7,000 after-tax one. Pro-rata aggregation finds 100% after-tax. The full $7,000 conversion is tax-free.

The 401(k) trick works because 401(k) balances are NOT aggregated into the pro-rata formula. Only Traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs count. 401(k)s and Roth IRAs are ignored.

File Form 8606 every year. That form is the paper trail that proves to the IRS you already paid the tax on the $7,000.

Full notes, the math, and the Form 8606 walkthrough at compoundlane.com.
