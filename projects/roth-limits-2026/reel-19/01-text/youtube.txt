DESCRIPTION FULL (200-500 words):
The SECURE Act (Dec 2019) and SECURE Act 2.0 (Dec 2022) changed the rules for non-spouse beneficiaries of inherited IRAs. The 10-year rule is now the default (per SECURE 2.0 §401 and IRC §401(a)(9)):

If you inherit a Roth IRA from someone other than your spouse, you must empty the account by December 31 of the 10th year following the original owner's death. The Roth is still tax-free on the way out — but the empty-by deadline is hard. Miss it, and the IRS imposes a 25% excise tax on the amount that should have been withdrawn (reduced to 10% if corrected timely — per SECURE 2.0).

Who is exempt from the 10-year rule:
- Surviving spouses (can roll to their own IRA, use their own RMD age)
- Minor children of the account owner (until they reach the age of majority)
- Disabled or chronically ill beneficiaries
- Beneficiaries who are less than 10 years younger than the deceased

Two strategies for everyone else:

Strategy 1 — Lump sum in year 9. Wait until year 9, then withdraw the entire balance in one distribution. Maximum tax-free growth window. Drawback: one year of large income could push you into a higher tax bracket for unrelated income (Social Security, dividends, RSU vest).

Strategy 2 — Spread over years 1-10. Take ~10% per year. A $500,000 inherited Roth becomes $50,000 annual withdrawals, all tax-free. Smooths the tax impact. Loses some tax-free growth on the withdrawn dollars, but avoids the year-9 spike.

The Roth's tax-free nature is a major advantage here. A $500,000 traditional IRA inherited under the same rule would create the same tax bill — but the $50,000 annual withdrawal is fully taxable as ordinary income, not tax-free.

The bottom line: pull together a strategy with your CPA before year 1, not year 9.
