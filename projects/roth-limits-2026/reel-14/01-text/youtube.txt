DESCRIPTION FULL (200-500 words):
The compounding gap between starting at 25 and starting at 30 is not a five-year catch-up. It's a five-year compounding hole you will never recover.

The math, at a 7% real return and a $7,500 annual contribution (per IRS IR-2025-111):

The early starter (age 25 to 65, 40 years):
- Total contributions: 40 × $7,500 = $300,000
- Future value at age 65: about $1,985,000
- Growth on contributions: about $1,685,000

The late starter (age 30 to 65, 35 years):
- Total contributions: 35 × $7,500 = $262,500
- Future value at age 65: about $1,196,000
- Growth on contributions: about $933,000

Difference at age 65: roughly $1.6 million in favor of the early starter. The late starter contributes $37,500 less over their lifetime — and ends up with $789,000 less in growth.

Why the gap is so large: the first five years of contributions have 40 years to compound, not 35. The lost compounding on the lost contributions is itself compounding, so the gap widens every year.

The "catch-up" myth. To match the 25-year-old by 65, a 30-year-old must contribute roughly 25% more per year, every year, for the rest of their working life. That's $9,375 a year instead of $7,500 — on top of the catch-up they're already behind on. Most people cannot and do not make this adjustment.

The bottom line: starting late is the most expensive financial mistake most young adults will make. The fix is not "doubling down later." The fix is starting now, with whatever you can.
