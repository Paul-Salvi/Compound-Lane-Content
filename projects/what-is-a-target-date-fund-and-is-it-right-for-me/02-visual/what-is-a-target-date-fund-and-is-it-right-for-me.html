target date funds: 6 things to know

Target date funds:
right for me?

1

One fund, auto-rebalanced

A target date fund is one fund that owns a mix of stocks and bonds and automatically shifts toward bonds as you age. Pick the year you plan to retire — a 2060 fund for someone 30 today, a 2050 fund at 40 — and the fund does the rest.

✳ tip —
Pick the fund matching the year you turn 65–67. A 30-year-old in 2026 → 2060 fund. A 40-year-old → 2050 fund.
2

The glide path

Vanguard's 2060 fund holds roughly 90% stocks at age 30, sliding to about 50% at retirement age, then continuing down to roughly 30% by 7 years after retirement. Maximum growth early, less risk as you near the money, and enough growth left to last.

3

Index vs active fees

Index target date funds cost 0.08% to 0.12% per year. The actively managed versions run 0.60%+ — nearly 8x the cost with no guarantee of better returns. On a $50,000 balance, that's $40/year vs $315/year for the same product class.

Fee math over 30 years

The fee gap compounds. $50,000 growing at 7% for 30 years with a 0.08% fee ends at roughly $217,000. With a 0.63% fee the same balance ends at roughly $190,000 — about $27,000 less, all from the fee, no other change (hypothetical, constant 7%).

5

Target date vs DIY

For investors under ~$200K, the ~$200/year extra cost on a $500K balance buys automation and behavioral guardrails. The trade flips for large portfolios spread across taxable and tax-advantaged accounts — a DIY three-fund portfolio gives full control over tax placement.

6

Common mistakes

Don't own two target date funds — owning a 2055 and a 2040 breaks the glide path. Don't mix target date with DIY in the same account. Don't pick by the year you started investing — the date is the risk dial, not a sentimental marker.

✳ watch out —
One target date fund per account. A 25-year-old in a 2030 fund gives up decades of growth it won't get back. The date is the risk dial.
