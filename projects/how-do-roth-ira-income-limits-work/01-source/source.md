## What the 2026 numbers are {#what-the-2026-numbers-are}

This is the snapshot to save. The [Retirement Accounts](/retirement-accounts) hub tracks all 2026 retirement figures — these are the Roth IRA pieces.

| Item | 2026 figure | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Roth IRA contribution limit (under 50) | **$7,500**<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-1">[1]</a></span> | Max across all IRAs |
| Catch-up at 50+ | **$8,600**<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-1">[1]</a></span> | $7,500 + $1,100 extra |
| 401(k) elective deferral limit | **$24,500** ($32,500 at 50+)<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-2">[2]</a></span> | Separate from IRA; you can do both |
| Full Roth if single MAGI | **under $153,000**<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span> | Full $7,500 ($8,600 if 50+) |

You also need earned income at least equal to what you contribute. Earn $5,000 in 2026 and your cap is $5,000 even though the posted limit is $7,500<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-1">[1]</a></span>.

## What MAGI means and why it controls your limit {#what-magi-means}

<span class="term" data-def="Modified Adjusted Gross Income — your adjusted gross income plus certain add-backs; the IRS measure for Roth limits.">MAGI</span> is not your salary. It is the tax definition the IRS uses to decide who qualifies.

Start with **Adjusted Gross Income (AGI)** from your return. Then the IRS adds back a few items for the Roth test — mainly foreign earned income exclusion, foreign housing amounts, and excluded income from Puerto Rico or American Samoa<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-4">[4]</a></span>. For most people without those exclusions, MAGI equals AGI.

Why it matters: pre-tax 401(k) contributions lower AGI and thus MAGI, while a year-end bonus or freelance income can push you over a threshold even if base salary is under it. Filing status also changes the range you compare to<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span>.

If you are close to a limit, estimate MAGI before you contribute. Fixing an excess contribution later means extra paperwork you can avoid.

## The phase-out: full, partial, or none {#the-phase-out}

The <span class="term" data-def="The income range where the IRS gradually reduces your allowed Roth contribution from full to zero.">phase-out</span> is a ramp, not a cliff. Below the bottom you get the full amount. Inside, your limit shrinks proportionally. At or above the top you get zero.

The windows are narrow — **$15,000 for single** and **$10,000 for joint**<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span> — so a $5,000 raise inside the window cuts a third to a half of your allowed amount.

Here is the full picture for 2026:

| Filing status | Full contribution | Partial (reduced) | No direct Roth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single | MAGI **under $153,000**<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span> | **$153,000 to under $168,000**<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span> | **$168,000+**<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span> |
| Married filing jointly | MAGI **under $242,000**<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span> | **$242,000 to under $252,000**<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span> | **$252,000+**<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span> |
| Married filing separately | **$0–$10,000** only<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span> | **$0–$10,000** reduced to zero at $10,000<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span> | **$10,000+**<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span> |

The takeaway: single at $160,000 MAGI is about halfway through the $15K window — expect roughly half the limit. Joint at $247,000 is halfway through the $10K window — same idea.

Married filing separately is the outlier: **$0 to $10,000**<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span>, which blocks direct Roth contributions for most couples who file separately.

## How to figure a partial contribution {#how-to-figure-a-partial-contribution}

Inside the phase-out the IRS uses a fraction: how far you are from the top, divided by the range size, times the limit.

For single in 2026:

- Range size = $168,000 − $153,000 = **$15,000**
- Allowed = ($168,000 − your MAGI) ÷ $15,000 × **$7,500** (or $8,600 if 50+)<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span>

For married joint:

- Range size = $252,000 − $242,000 = **$10,000**
- Allowed = ($252,000 − your MAGI) ÷ $10,000 × **$7,500**<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span>

Round up to the next $10; the IRS bumps a small positive result to at least $200 in some cases.

**Example — single, MAGI $160,500:** ($168,000 − $160,500) = $7,500. $7,500 ÷ $15,000 = **0.50**. 0.50 × $7,500 = **$3,750** allowed — half the window, half the contribution. Every dollar inside the window reduces what you can add.

## What to do if you are over the limit {#what-to-do-if-you-are-over}

Over $168,000 single or $252,000 joint, you cannot contribute directly<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span> — but Roth-style saving is not over. Three paths:

1. **<span class="term" data-def="Contributing to a traditional IRA and then converting it to a Roth IRA — a workaround when income is too high for a direct Roth contribution.">backdoor Roth</span> contribution.** Contribute to a traditional IRA (no income limit to contribute) and convert to Roth<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-5">[5]</a></span>. The conversion is taxable on any pre-tax dollars, and the pro-rata rule aggregates all traditional IRAs — so a large pre-tax IRA makes most of the conversion taxable.

2. **Max the 401(k).** The 2026 elective limit is **$24,500** ($32,500 at 50+)<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-2">[2]</a></span> with no income phase-out for Roth 401(k) contributions. If you have a workplace plan, filling it to the match is often the higher-dollar move.

3. **Use a taxable brokerage for overflow.** No limits, no MAGI test. You lose tax-free growth but keep flexibility and the same low-cost funds.

Your 401(k) and Roth IRA caps are separate. Maxing one does not reduce the other — you can do **$24,500** plus **$7,500** in the same year if you qualify<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-1">[1]</a></span><span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-2">[2]</a></span>.

For sequencing — match first, then Roth, then back to the 401(k) — the [401(k) or Roth IRA? tool](/401k-vs-roth-ira) walks the priority order.

## How this fits your retirement plan {#how-this-fits-your-plan}

The income limit is a filter on *how* you get Roth dollars, not whether a Roth is good. Under the floor, the direct Roth is simplest: after-tax in, tax-free growth, tax-free qualified withdrawals. That is why [how to open a Roth IRA step by step](/how-to-open-a-roth-ira-step-by-step) starts there — fund up to $7,500, buy a broad index fund, automate.

Inside the phase-out, the partial still compounds. At 7% for 30 years, **$3,750/year** grows to roughly **$354,000** — half the **~$708,000** from $7,500 (hypothetical, constant return). The same lesson from [what is an expense ratio and how does it eat returns](/what-is-an-expense-ratio-and-how-does-it-eat-returns) applies: consistent contributions beat sporadic ones because time does the work.

Over the top, weigh the backdoor's tax and reporting cost against adding to a Roth 401(k). The [Roth vs Traditional IRA](/roth-vs-traditional) tool models tax now versus later, and [what is a target date fund and is it right for me](/what-is-a-target-date-fund-and-is-it-right-for-me) helps with what to buy once funded. Estimate MAGI in the fall — a bonus can still push you into the window with time to adjust.

## FAQ {#faq}

<div class="faq-source">
## FAQ

**How do Roth IRA income limits work for 2026?**
If MAGI is under $153,000 single or $242,000 joint, you can contribute the full $7,500 ($8,600 at 50+). Inside $153K–$168K single or $242K–$252K joint the IRS shrinks your limit proportionally to zero. At $168K+ single or $252K+ joint you cannot contribute directly.

**What counts as MAGI for Roth IRA purposes?**
Modified Adjusted Gross Income — AGI plus add-backs for foreign earned income, foreign housing, and certain excluded income. For most people without those items, MAGI equals AGI. See IRS Publication 590-A.

**What if my income falls inside the phase-out range?**
You get a reduced contribution: (top − your MAGI) ÷ range × $7,500, rounded to the next $10. Single at $160,500 is halfway through a $15K window — about $3,750 allowed.

**Can I use a backdoor Roth if I am over the income limit?**
You can contribute to a traditional IRA and convert to Roth. The conversion is taxable on pre-tax dollars and the pro-rata rule counts all traditional IRAs together. It works best with little pre-tax IRA balance.

**Do Roth IRA and 401(k) limits affect each other?**
No. The 2026 Roth IRA limit is $7,500 and the 401(k) elective limit is $24,500 — separate caps. You can max both if you have the earned income.

**How do Roth IRA income limits work if I am married filing separately?**
The phase-out is $0 to $10,000 — essentially no direct Roth contribution is allowed above $10,000 MAGI when filing separately.

</div>

## Bottom line {#bottom-line}

In 2026 the Roth IRA test is a MAGI check with a narrow ramp: full under **$153K single / $242K joint**, fading to zero by **$168K / $252K**, on top of the **$7,500 ($8,600 at 50+)** cap<span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-1">[1]</a></span><span class="src-ref"><a href="#source-3">[3]</a></span>.
