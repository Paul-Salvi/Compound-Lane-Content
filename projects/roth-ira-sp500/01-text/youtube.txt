The Roth IRA is the single best deal in personal finance — and almost
nobody talks about why it beats every other account on the menu.

You put in money you've already paid taxes on. It grows for decades.
And on the way out, you never owe a penny of tax on the growth. Not
on dividends. Not on capital gains. Not on withdrawal. That's not a
fee structure — that's a tax shelter, and it's the most generous one
the IRS offers to working Americans.

But there's a catch. The contribution limit is seven thousand dollars
a year for 2024, and it's earned income only. No 1099, no Roth.
Side gigs, salary, and self-employment all count.

So is it worth maxing out? Here's the math. Seven thousand a year,
compounded at seven percent for thirty years, lands at roughly a
million dollars. Tax free. That's the compounding engine no taxable
account can match — because every dollar of growth in a taxable
account gets taxed every year, and the drag compounds.

How to set it up in three steps:
1. Open a Vanguard account (skip the trading apps — the fee difference
   is real).
2. Fund it with VOO — the S and P 500 at 0.03 percent expense ratio.
3. Set it on autopilot and don't touch it till fifty nine and a half.

Educational content, not financial advice.

Sources: IRS Publication 590-A (2024), Vanguard VOO expense ratio
disclosure, S&P 500 historical returns 1928-2025.
