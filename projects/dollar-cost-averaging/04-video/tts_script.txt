Six things to know about DCA — dollar-cost averaging.

DCA means investing the same dollars on a schedule — not all at once.
Like eight thirty three each month, for twelve months, to deploy ten thousand dollars.
You buy fewer shares when prices are high. More when they're low.

Vanguard and Schwab tested rolling twelve-month windows going back to the nineteen twenties.
Lump sum beat twelve-month DCA about sixty eight percent of the time.
The reason is simple — markets rise more often than they fall.

Ten thousand dollars invested day one grows to about ten thousand seven hundred after twelve months.
DCA at end-of-month — the same ten thousand split into twelve checks — grows to about ten thousand three hundred twenty seven.
That's cash drag, not a fee.

Lump sum earns a full year on the whole ten thousand.
DCA at end-of-month earns about half a year on average.
Your first check earns roughly eleven months. Your last one — almost zero.

So when does DCA actually help? When you'd otherwise sit in cash from fear — or when markets drop right after.
At least you didn't deploy everything before the fall.
When does it hurt? Whenever markets rise while your cash waits. That's most years.

How to use DCA right. Automate your paycheck investing on payday — there's no drag, because the money was never in cash.
For a windfall, invest promptly — or DCA a short window. Three to six months, max.
Park any waiting cash in a high-yield account. And check your target, not the entry price.

That's dollar-cost averaging.
